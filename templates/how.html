School money

How TEEOSA turns need into aid

TEEOSA, the Tax Equity and Educational Opportunities Support Act, is Nebraska's main formula for state aid to schools. For {{ aid_year }} it pays {{ aid_total }} to {{ n_sys }} school systems. It works in three moves:

  1. Need. The formula sets what each system needs: basic funding per student plus allowances for poverty, English learners, transportation and other costs. Statewide: {{ need_total }}.
  2. Resources. It counts what each system can raise or already receives, mostly local effort: $1.00 for every $100 of the system's school adjusted valuation. Statewide: {{ res_total }}.
  3. The gap. Where need is larger than resources, the state pays the gap as equalization aid. {{ n_eq }} of {{ n_sys }} systems receive it, {{ eq_total }} in all. Every system also receives foundation aid under LB 583 (2023), {{ found_total }} in all, plus smaller payments.

Statewide, resources ({{ res_total }}) are larger than need ({{ need_total }}) because systems with high school adjusted valuation per student count far more resources than they need. The gap that equalization aid fills exists only system by system.

Taxable valuation and school adjusted valuation, used for different things

Taxable valuation is the value of taxable property within a school district, as assessed by county assessors. It is the property tax base for the district's actual levy, expressed per $100 of taxable valuation. Every levy, tax bill and credit uses it.

School adjusted valuation is the value of taxable property within a school district, adjusted so values are measured consistently across the state: 96% of actual value for most real property and 72% for agricultural land. TEEOSA uses it to estimate local property tax resources (local effort, $1.00 for every $100). It is used only in the formula; no one is taxed on it.

For {{ aid_year }} the two are close statewide, {{ tv_total }} taxable and {{ sav_total }} school adjusted, but they can differ for a single system. {{ lx.short }}: {{ lex_tv }} taxable and {{ lex_sav }} school adjusted per formula student.

Where property taxes and credits fit

Each district sets its own property tax request. LB 243 (2023) caps its request authority at 3% growth a year plus allowances, and a vote of at least 70% of the board can raise the growth percentage by 4 to 7 points, depending on enrollment. The request divided by taxable valuation sets the levy. The General Fund and Special Building Fund levies together are generally limited to $1.05 per $100 of taxable valuation, plus any override voters approve; qualifying bond levies are outside the limit. For {{ budget_year }}, {{ votes }} of {{ n_budgets }} boards used that vote, and districts left {{ unused }} of authority unrequested. State property tax credits then pay part of each tax bill: {{ credit_share }} of all property taxes in tax year {{ y1 }}.

How students are counted

  • Formula students: the student count used in the TEEOSA calculation, enrollment adjusted for historical ADM trends, including qualifying early childhood students and students the district pays tuition for. Used for every aid and need measure.
  • Average daily membership (ADM): the average number of students enrolled through the school year, not a single-day count. Used for spending and money received.
  • Students living in a county: an estimate that splits each district by where its children live. Used for county school figures.

Testing

How the Relationships tab tells a pattern from chance

Chance shows up when you look at many things

Compare one measure with dozens of others and a few will line up by luck. Each measure page corrects for the number of comparisons, and marks the relationships that survive as stronger than chance.

Some measures move together by arithmetic

Aid per student and school adjusted valuation per student are tied by the formula itself. Pairs like that are explained first, under Why, by design, and are not tested as findings.

Ranks, not raw values

Relationships are measured on rank order, so one extreme system or county cannot create a pattern on its own.

A relationship is not a cause

Two measures can rise together because something else drives both. Each measure page says what that might be, or that no formula links them, before it shows the relationship.
